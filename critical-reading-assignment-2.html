Barriers and Breakthroughs - Critical Reading of Mikalah Guyton's "Gangsta Rap and the Trapped Mentality"

A$AP Ferg's "Plain Jane" is an example of the evolution of rap music over time, as it  adapts pays homage to influential rap groups of the past while perpetuating problematic themes. Mikalah Guyton's analysis in "Gangsta Rap and the Trapped Mentality" provides a framework to understand how the common themes of "(1) masculinity, (2) respect, (3) violence and retaliation, (4) misogyny, (5) wealth, and (6) drugs" (Guyton 2021, 1) emerged and have come to define both gangsta rap and trap music. While the genre provides a route to success for black artists from marginalized backgrounds, they also promote potentially harmful messages to their audience. Guyton's concept of the "trapped mentality" examines this balance of success and toxicity, a sentiment that is perpetuated clearly in Ferg's own lyrics.

Drawing from other music academics, Guyton first outlines how hip-hop as a genre began in the 1970s from outdoor parties that took place in a destroyed Bronx neighborhood as a way to promote community and peace, and then how rap became a natural supplement to hip-hop’s flowy beats (Guyton 2021, 5). Guyton's historical analysis then traces rap's mainstream evolution through the 1980s crack epidemic, demonstrating how this societal crisis redirected the genre toward expressing personal struggles and advocating for change within Black communities (Guyton 2021, 10).

Guyton constructed a compelling historical narrative about evolution of themes in trap music by presenting key historical events as causes for the genre's development. Her inclusion of direct lyrical examples from contemporary rap songs added strong evidence for her analysis. She also combines this with direct lyrical examples from rap songs of the time to supplement her argument, which I believe provides even more strength to her argument. This helps to build a sort of inductive argument, where one event builds on the other, to show the growth and evolution of rap from its origin to today. For example, Guyton discusses how, because of the crack epidemic, the LA police would crack down heavily on minority communities, which inspired the massively popular N.W.A. song “Fuck tha Police,” followed by “Fuck the police … They have the authority to kill a minority”, which directly relates to her argument (Guyton 2021, 11).

Additionally, the author strengthened her argument by addressing some counter arguments to her claim. Although such genres of music perpetuated some problematic values, their songs could help support struggling communities and inspire individuals in such communities to try and break out of the place they seem to be trapped in (Guyton 2021, 13). However, there is very little discussion about the positive impacts of rap, such as its role in creating opportunities for struggling individuals in low income communities, as well as its role in promoting community–not only historically but also now. As Guyton states: “Trap music chooses to glamorize the trapping lifestyle, which sometimes leaves little room for the installment of positive messages to its listeners” (Guyton 2021, 28). 

Throughout the reading, Guyton also presents the idea of the “trapped mentality” that many artists and individuals in the genre have. She elaborates on this by saying “Many trappers escaped the trap by pursuing rap careers, and continue to demonstrate their self-reliant mentality to progress in their new profession. Unfortunately, due to the continuous glamorization of their trap lifestyle and urge to attain this standard of manhood, these artists will remain mentally trapped in this reality” (Guyton 2021, 30). She also argues how, to say relevant, artists "continue to lyrically reflect and maintain their trapping personas in efforts to identify with Trap artists and remain relevant in Trap culture" (Guyton 2021, 29). This is reflected across the genre with songs with common lyrics talking about drugs and glow-ups across many songs, including A$AP Ferg’s “Plain Jane”. While this indeed highlights real-world struggles that affect African-American communities, it perpetuates a hyper-masculine and problematic message to the audiences of this music.

Mikalah Guyton's analysis reveals a duality of rap: while it does provide escape routes from difficult circumstances for up-and-coming artists, it simultaneously creates a cycle that mentally traps artists within problematic themes of masculinity, drugs, and material success. This is particularly evident in Ferg's work, but his collaboration with Nicki Minaj (as discussed in the Music Review) attempts to challenge misogynistic traditions while still operating within of trap music's masculinity-centered expression. The argument presented has helped me gain a deeper understanding of the culture and history behind the genre, as well as some of the artists that A$AP Ferg took inspiration from as he grew up. While there is room for improvement, Guyton uses a strong framework to present information and make logical inferences, ultimately providing an insightful piece into rap history and motifs, things generally hidden behind strong lyrics of struggle and success. Such findings are essential for my broader project of examining how modern trap artists (such as Ferg) both perpetuate and occasionally transcend the genre's problematic foundations.
